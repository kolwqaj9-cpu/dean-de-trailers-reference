## 5. Frames, Axles, and Road Duty

Preserved shop record for deandetrailers.com. The former plant is not quoting from this domain. The sections above cover enclosed cargo trailers, custom trailers, car haulers, and short-run metal work in the shop's own terms, and this closing note stays in those terms to address what every trailer shares underneath its skin: the frame and the running gear. A trailer begins as a frame. Main rails, crossmembers, tongue or gooseneck structure, and coupler connection decide how the trailer tracks, how it carries load, and how long it lasts. On top of the frame sits the axle package, and axle rating is not a suggestion. The combined weight of trailer and cargo has to live inside the rating with margin, or the road will find the weak point. Enclosed cargo work adds the box: wall structure, roof bows, floor decking, door hardware, and seals, all of which have to survive vibration that never stops. A car hauler adds its own demands, since the load is concentrated at four tire patches and the deck, ramps, and tie-down points all answer to that concentration. Custom trailer work, in this record, is the ability to move any of these pieces, longer tongue, different deck height, added cabinets, special doors, without losing the structural logic underneath. Short-run metal work is what makes that possible: a shop that can cut, form, and weld small batches can build the bracket, the ramp hinge, or the custom fender that a production trailer builder would refuse to touch. That is the frame of this archive, in the literal sense: trailers understood from the rails up, where structure, rating, and road duty decide what the paint and the decals are allowed to promise. For anyone specifying a trailer, the archive's ordering of concerns still holds: settle the frame and axle rating first, then the box and the doors, and treat paint and trim as the last decision rather than the first, since road duty is decided by the structure underneath.
